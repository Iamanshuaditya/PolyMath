# Paper Library

This is a selective library of papers/reviews worth understanding. It is not a citation dump. Start with accessible reviews; move to technical originals when prerequisites are in place.

## Learning science

### S — Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques
- Authors: John Dunlosky, Katherine Rawson, Elizabeth Marsh, Mitchell Nathan, Daniel Willingham
- Year: 2013
- Type: REVIEW / ACADEMIC
- Difficulty: BEGINNER ACCESSIBLE
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/
- Why: compares ten common study techniques and gives especially strong support to practice testing and distributed practice.
- Epistemic status: strong review of the literature at publication; individual techniques should still be updated with later research.

### A — Self-Regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions
- Authors: Robert Bjork, John Dunlosky, Nate Kornell
- Year: 2013
- Type: REVIEW
- Difficulty: BEGINNER ACCESSIBLE
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23020639/
- Why: explains why subjective feelings of fluency can mislead learners.

### S — Test-Enhanced Learning
- Authors: Henry Roediger III & Jeffrey Karpicke
- Year: 2006
- Type: EXPERIMENTAL / LANDMARK
- Difficulty: BEGINNER/INTERMEDIATE
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- Why: landmark demonstration of retrieval practice benefits for long-term retention.

### S — Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis
- Authors: Cepeda et al.
- Year: 2006
- Type: META-ANALYSIS
- Difficulty: INTERMEDIATE
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
- Why: major quantitative synthesis of spacing effects.

## Judgment, decision-making and behavioral science

### S — Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk
- Authors: Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky
- Year: 1979
- Type: LANDMARK ORIGINAL PAPER
- Difficulty: INTERMEDIATE
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185

## How to read a paper

1. Read title/abstract and predict the claim.
2. Identify the research question.
3. Identify design/data/assumptions.
4. Write the strongest result in plain language.
5. Separate what was measured from what authors infer.
6. Find limitations and later replications/critiques.
7. Revisit methods/equations only as deeply as the goal requires.
8. Record confidence and what would change your mind.

The goal is not to say "I read the paper." The goal is to know what the paper actually establishes—and what it does not.
